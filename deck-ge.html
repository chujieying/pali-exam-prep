Question 1Social factors behind the rapid spread of Buddhism in the Buddha's time

Principal's cue

“Definitely one question.” The caste system (people were tired of it; the Buddha rejected it). Expensive, ritualistic religion wasting time and wealth. Support of the khattiyas and kings.

Memory trick: the social factors“Caste, Cost, Crown, Coins, Curiosity, Compassion”

Against caste · against costly rituals · crown (kings, khattiyas) · coins (merchants) · curiosity (free inquiry) · compassion (the Buddha's mission).

Memory trick: the first patrons“Bamboo, Jet, East”

Bimbisāra's Bamboo grove (Veḷuvana) · Anāthapiṇḍika's Jetavana · Visākhā's Eastern monastery (Pubbārāma).

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Within 45 years of teaching, Buddhism spread across the Ganges plain and drew kings, merchants, brahmins and outcastes. This was due to the social conditions of 6th-century BCE India and to the Buddha and his Saṅgha.

Body

  1. Against caste: people were tired of birth-based discrimination; the Buddha rejected it and ordained all (Upāli the barber, Sunīta the scavenger, women).
  2. Against costly rituals: Brahmanical sacrifices wasted time and wealth; the Buddha offered a path of morality, meditation and wisdom with no priests.
  3. Kings and khattiyas: Bimbisāra gave the Veḷuvana, the first monastery; Pasenadi became a follower; rising khattiyas welcomed a teaching that didn't put brahmins on top.
  4. Merchants and cities: Anāthapiṇḍika (Jetavana) and Visākhā supported the Saṅgha; traders carried the teaching along routes.
  5. Free inquiry: an age of new teachers; the Buddha invited people to test the teaching (Kālāma Sutta).
  6. The Buddha and the Saṅgha: taught in local languages with similes; sent out sixty arahants “for the welfare of the many”; great disciples spread it.

Conclusion

Buddhism spread fast because it met the social needs of its time (rejecting caste and costly ritual, winning kings and merchants) and offered a rational path open to all, spread by an organised Saṅgha.

Question 2The Buddhist Councils and how Theravāda and Mahāyāna arose

Principal's cue

Memory trick: the 2nd Council numbers“100 · 700 · 10 · 8”

100 years after · 700 arahants · 10 wrong points · 8 judges. Vesālī, King Kālāsoka.

Memory trick: the four councils“Recite, Repair, Remove, Record”

1st recited the teaching · 2nd repaired the Vinaya · 3rd removed false monks · 4th recorded it on palm leaves.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

With no successor (“the Dhamma and Vinaya will be your teacher”), the Saṅgha held councils to protect the teaching and settle disputes.

Body

  1. First Council: three months after the Parinibbāna, Sattapaṇṇi cave, Rājagaha; Ajātasattu; 500 arahants under Mahākassapa, after Subhadda's remark. Upāli recited the Vinaya, Ānanda the Dhamma; collections given to groups to memorise.
  2. Second Council (most important): ~100 years later at Vesālī under Kālāsoka, 700 arahants. The Vajjian monks' ten points, above all accepting money; Yasa protested; an eight-monk committee (4 east, 4 west) rejected all ten.
  3. The first split: Sthaviras (elders) kept the tradition; Mahāsāṅghikas (the larger group) wanted change; later 18 schools. Rituals came in after this. Not Theravāda vs Mahāyāna.
  4. Third Council: ~236 years after, Pāṭaliputta, Asoka, Moggaliputta Tissa; Vibhajjavāda accepted, ~60,000 false monks expelled; Kathāvatthu (first Abhidhamma mention); nine missions abroad.
  5. Fourth Council: Theravāda: Āluvihāra, Sri Lanka (1st c. BCE), Tipiṭaka written on palm leaves; the northern one in Kashmir under Kaniṣka.
  6. Theravāda & Mahāyāna: later, those aiming at Buddhahood called themselves Mahāyāna (and others Hīnayāna); the elders' tradition took the name Theravāda. It happened gradually.

Conclusion

The councils preserved the Dhamma and Vinaya: recite, repair, remove, record. The Second Council protected discipline but saw the first split, between elders and Mahāsāṅghikas, not Theravāda and Mahāyāna.

Question 3King Asoka's contribution to the global and sustained spread of Buddhism

Principal's cue

“Pay more attention to King Asoka: global spread and sustained development.” A critical angle: after Kalinga he gave up war but used Buddhist envoys to extend influence. “One way it is a better thing, but the other way…”

Memory trick: his contributions“Repent, Rule, Build, Purify, Send”

Repent after Kalinga · rule by Dhamma · build stūpas and hospitals · purify the Saṅgha · send missions abroad.

Memory trick: global vs sustained“Spread wide, rooted deep”

Wide: nine missions and Greek envoys. Deep: purified Saṅgha, complete canon, local Saṅghas that outlived Indian Buddhism.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Asoka, the third Mauryan emperor, ruled almost all of India from Pāṭaliputta. Caṇḍāsoka (“the fierce”) became Dhammāsoka (“the righteous”) after the Kalinga war: the greatest royal patron in Buddhist history.

Body

  1. Conversion: remorse after Kalinga (100,000 killed, 150,000 deported) led him to Dhammavijaya, conquest by righteousness.
  2. Within India: 84,000 stūpas (tradition); pilgrimages (Lumbinī pillar); rock and pillar edicts on morality and tolerance; Dhamma officers; animal sacrifice banned; hospitals for people and animals, wells, trees.
  3. Global spread: sponsored the Third Council, after which nine missions went to Kashmir, Gandhāra, the Himalayas, the Deccan, Greek lands, Suvaṇṇabhūmi and Sri Lanka; envoys to five Greek kings; sent his son Mahinda and daughter Saṅghamittā with the Bodhi sapling.
  4. Sustained development: purified the Saṅgha so orthodox teaching went abroad; completed the canon with the Kathāvatthu; missions set up local Saṅghas that survived after India; became the model Buddhist king.
  5. Critical view: his missions also served politics, gaining influence over neighbours without war. Good for Buddhism, but also good for Asoka.

Conclusion

Asoka governed by Dhamma, purified the Saṅgha and sent missions across Asia, explaining both the wide spread and the lasting roots of Buddhism. Without him Theravāda might not have survived outside India.

Memory trick: Buddhaghosa“Back to Pāli, the Path of Purity”

He translated the Sinhala commentaries back into Pāli and wrote the Visuddhimagga (Path of Purification).

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Ven. Mahinda, son of Asoka, ordained at 20 and an arahant, was sent to Sri Lanka after the Third Council. He waited, staying in southern India, until Sri Lanka was stable, then arrived at Mihintale on Poson full moon (c. 250 BCE), in the reign of Devānampiya Tissa.

Body

  1. The king first: met the king while hunting, tested him with the mango-tree questions, taught the Cūḷahatthipadopama Sutta; the king and followers took refuge.
  2. Teaching the people: simple stories of good and bad deeds (Petavatthu, Vimānavatthu).
  3. Institutions: the Mahāmeghavana became the Mahāvihāra; the Thūpārāma stūpa for relics; Saṅghamittā brought the Bodhi sapling and founded the nuns' Order.
  4. A native Saṅgha: Sri Lankans ordained; Buddhism “established” when Ariṭṭha, born in Lanka, recited the Vinaya there.
  5. Language and literature: commentaries in Sinhala developed the written language; the Tipiṭaka was written down at Āluvihāra (1st c. BCE).
  6. Buddhaghosa (5th c. CE): came from India to the Mahāvihāra, wrote the Visuddhimagga and put the Sinhala commentaries into Pāli: the standard for Theravāda everywhere.

Conclusion

Mahinda's wisdom and patience rooted Buddhism in Sri Lanka through the king, the people, lasting institutions and a native Saṅgha. With Buddhaghosa's work, Sri Lanka became the centre of Theravāda.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Theravāda has shaped Thailand since Sukhothai and Ayutthaya, whose kings adopted the Sri Lankan tradition. In the modern era King Chulalongkorn (Rama V, 1868–1910) organised the Saṅgha nationally through the Sangha Administration Act of R.E. 121 (1902), making Thailand the most systematically organised Theravāda country.

Body

  1. Background: Ram Khamhaeng invited Sri Lankan-lineage monks; Borommakot sent Upāli to restore ordination in Sri Lanka (1753); Rama IV founded the Dhammayuttika Nikāya, giving two orders.
  2. Why the Act: Rama V was modernising the country; regions ran the Saṅgha differently. The preamble: an orderly Saṅgha makes the religion “prosperous, long-lasting” and benefits the kingdom.
  3. Features: issued by royal decree; a national ladder of wat → district → province → region; rules for monasteries, sīmā and abbots; discipline and appeals.
  4. Governance: no Supreme Patriarch role in the Act; the King as patron governed, advised by a Council of Elders.
  5. Results: a unified Saṅgha supporting national unity; standardised monastic education and exams; the Mahamakut and Mahachulalongkorn universities.
  6. Later Acts: 1941 (parliament-style, caused conflict) and 1962 (centralised under the Supreme Patriarch, still in force).

Conclusion

Rama V's 1902 Act turned the Thai Saṅgha into a unified national organisation with a clear hierarchy and education system, strengthening both Buddhism and the nation.

Memory trick: what Anawrahta did“Shin, Suppress, Seize, Shwezigon, Share”

Converted by Shin Arahan · suppressed the Ari · seized the Tipiṭaka from Thaton · began the Shwezigon · shared monks with Sri Lanka.

Memory trick: the year“Ten-Fifty-Seven, Tipiṭaka to Pagan”

1057: the conquest of Thaton brought the scriptures to Pagan.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Buddhism reached Myanmar early through merchants and missionaries, but became firmly Theravāda through King Anawrahta of Pagan (1044–1077), the founder of Theravāda Myanmar.

Body

  1. Before him: tradition of the merchants Tapussa and Bhalluka (hair relics, Shwedagon); Soṇa and Uttara to Thaton; Pyu Pāli inscriptions; the Mon of Thaton as a Theravāda centre.
  2. The problem: in Upper Myanmar, corrupt Ari monks and mixed Mahāyāna, Tantric and Brahmanical beliefs.
  3. Conversion and reform: converted by Shin Arahan, a Mon monk; suppressed the Ari; made Theravāda the state religion.
  4. Thaton (1057): when King Manuha refused the Tipiṭaka, Anawrahta conquered Thaton and brought texts, monks and artisans to Pagan.
  5. Building and Sri Lanka: began the Shwezigon; got texts and a Tooth relic replica from Sri Lanka; sent monks to restore Sri Lanka's ordination after the Chola invasion.
  6. Legacy: Kyansittha (Ānanda temple), the Fifth Council at Mandalay (1871), the Sixth in Yangon (1954–56); about 90% Buddhist today.

Conclusion

Royal patronage, above all Anawrahta's, made Theravāda the national religion of Myanmar, and later kings and links with Sri Lanka made it a leading guardian of Theravāda.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Laos is a landlocked, mountainous country on the Mekong. About two-thirds are Theravāda Buddhists, and it has been the state religion since King Fa Ngum founded Lan Xang (“Land of a Million Elephants”) in 1353. Lao Buddhism blends with older spirit beliefs.

Body

  1. Early influences: Theravāda from Dvāravatī; from Nan-Chao, the idea of the king as protector of Buddhism; Mahāyāna under Khmer rule.
  2. Fa Ngum: raised at Angkor by the monk Phra Mahā Pasāman, married a Khmer Buddhist princess, founded Lan Xang with Khmer help and made Theravāda the state religion.
  3. The mission: monks, the Tipiṭaka and the Phra Bang image came from Cambodia; Luang Prabang is named after it; a Bodhi tree was planted.
  4. Later kings: Photisarath banned spirit shrines and brought the Tipiṭaka from Lanna; Setthathirath built That Luang in Vientiane.
  5. Modern: French reforms; state control after 1975; revival since the 1980s; That Luang on the national seal.
  6. Practices today: tak bat (dawn alms of sticky rice); belief in spirits (phi) and spirit houses at temples; the baci ceremony, tying white strings on wrists to bind good spirits and ward off bad luck; temporary ordination; festivals (Vesak, Pi Mai, That Luang).

Conclusion

Theravāda came to Laos through Fa Ngum's mission and was strengthened by the kings of Lan Xang. Despite colonialism and communism, it unifies Lao culture, lived daily through alms, merit-making and festivals, blended with spirit beliefs.

Question 8The decline of Theravāda Buddhism, and lessons for today

Principal's cue

“The decline of Theravāda Buddhism.” From Lessons 6, 14 and 15b: decline in India, decline in Sri Lanka under colonial rule, weakening and sustenance.

Memory trick: internal causes“Split, Slack, Similar, Separate, Supported”

Split into schools · slack discipline · too similar to Hinduism · separate from the laity · over-supported by kings.

Memory trick: the lesson“Strong monks, smart laity”

Buddhism survives where the Saṅgha is disciplined and the lay people are educated and involved.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Buddhism flourished in India for ~1,700 years but almost vanished by the 13th century. Sri Lanka and other Theravāda lands also declined under invasion and colonial rule. The causes teach us how to sustain Buddhism today.

Body

  1. Internal causes: division into schools; moral laxity of some monks; loss of a distinct identity (Hindu-like rituals; the Buddha absorbed as an avatar of Vishnu); wealthy monasteries far from the laity; over-dependence on kings.
  2. External causes in India: Brahmanical revival and Bhakti; persecution (Pushyamitra, Śaśāṅka); invasions destroying Nālandā and Vikramaśilā (~1193–1203).
  3. Elsewhere: Sri Lanka: South Indian invasions; Portuguese, Dutch and British rule; no fully ordained monks by the 1750s until Thailand helped (1753). Laos and Cambodia: war and communism.
  4. Revival: Sri Lanka: pirivena colleges, the Panadura debate, Olcott's schools, Dharmapāla. India: the Maha Bodhi Society (1891), Ambedkar (1956).
  5. Lessons for today: disciplined Saṅgha; educated, engaged laity; clear identity with modern presentation; unity; not relying only on the state; social service; preserving texts and using modern media.

Conclusion

Theravāda declined where internal weakness met external blows, and survived where the Saṅgha stayed disciplined and the laity stayed educated and engaged.
