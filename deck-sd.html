Question 1The origin of society and social institutions (Aggañña Sutta)

Principal's cue

★ Class system and origin of society; social institutions; how the first house was built. Defilements started when social structure appeared. Don't focus only on caste: explain from the beginning.

Memory trick: the story in order“Food → Looks → Love → House → Field → Thief → King → Class”

Each step is caused by craving. That's the moral: society's problems start in the mind.

Memory trick: the first king's three names“Elected, Field-lord, Pleaser”

Mahāsammata (Great Elect) · Khattiya (lord of the fields) · Rājā (pleases others by Dhamma).

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The Aggañña Sutta (DN 27) is the Buddha's account of how society began, told to two young brahmins mocked for joining the Saṅgha. Against the claim that brahmins were born from Brahmā's mouth, he gave a gradual account: society arose from human craving and need, not divine creation.

Body

  1. The beginning: radiant, mind-made beings with no gender and no hierarchy.
  2. Craving and food: a greedy being tasted the earth-essence; as food became coarser, radiance faded and bodies became solid.
  3. Pride and jealousy: the good-looking became arrogant and the others jealous: the first defilements in social life.
  4. Family and the first house: gender and sexual relations appeared; criticised by others, couples built houses to hide, giving the household and family.
  5. Property and crime: people stored rice, the rice stopped regrowing, fields were divided (private property), then theft, lies and violence began.
  6. Government and classes: the people elected Mahāsammata to keep order for a share of rice (an early social contract). The four classes arose from occupations, not birth: “Dhamma is the best thing for people”.

Conclusion

The Aggañña Sutta shows social institutions (family, property, government, class) are human creations shaped by craving, so they can be reformed, and that conduct, not birth, makes a person noble.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The world faces global warming, pollution, deforestation and species loss. Buddhism sees the root as moral: greed, hatred and delusion. Born in the forest (Lumbinī, the Bodhi tree, Kusinārā's sāla trees), Buddhism treats humans as part of nature, not its masters.

Body

  1. Interdependence: “When this exists, that comes to be.” Because of sense desire, consumerism; because of consumerism, production; then extraction, emissions and climate change. Harming one part harms the whole.
  2. Impermanent nature, sped up by greed: nature always changes, but moral decline speeds harmful change (Aggañña; Cakkavatti-sīhanāda).
  3. Gratitude and friendship: the Buddha gazed at the Bodhi tree for a week in gratitude; don't break the branch that gave you shade.
  4. Precepts and mettā for all beings: the first precept; the Karaṇīya Metta Sutta; Asoka's hospitals for animals and tree planting.
  5. Vinaya rules: no cutting trees, no forest fires, no polluting water; robes reused down to plaster: early recycling.
  6. Simple, mindful living: the bee and the flower (Dhp 49); “cut the forest of craving, not the trees” (Dhp 283); planting trees is merit (Vanaropa Sutta). In practice: buy what you need, recycle, save energy, eat more plants.

Conclusion

Buddhism sees people and nature as interdependent. The crisis is caused by craving, so the solution starts with less craving and simple, mindful, compassionate living.

Memory trick: which direction“East = where life rises; West = where the day settles”

Memory trick: parents' five duties“Stop · Start · Skill · Spouse · Share”

Stop them from evil · start them on good · teach a skill · arrange a spouse · share the inheritance.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The family is the basic unit of society. In the Sigālovāda Sutta, the “householder's Vinaya”, the Buddha reinterpreted the six directions Sigāla worshipped as six relationships with mutual duties, the most important being family.

Body

  1. Children to parents (East): support them, do their duties, keep the family tradition, be worthy of the inheritance, make offerings in their memory.
  2. Parents to children: restrain them from evil, encourage good, train them in a profession, arrange a suitable marriage, hand over the inheritance. Parents are called “Brahmā” and “first teachers” (AN 4.63); supporting them is a highest blessing (Maṅgala Sutta).
  3. Husband to wife (West): honour her, don't look down on her, be faithful, give her authority at home, give her gifts.
  4. Wife to husband: do her duties well, be hospitable to both families, be faithful, protect the earnings, be skilled and hard-working. Reciprocal duties = partnership and equality.
  5. Lasting marriage: same faith, virtue, generosity and wisdom (Samajīvi Sutta); seven kinds of wives (Sujātā); the third precept protects trust.
  6. Today: elderly parents neglected, divorce, screen-addicted children: the same duties still apply.

Conclusion

Buddhism gives the family a central role. When parents, children and spouses fulfil the Sigālovāda Sutta's mutual duties, the family is happy and society is peaceful.

Memory trick: the first three“Give · Be good · Give up”

Answer skeleton

Introduction

In Buddhism the ruler is a servant of the people who rules by Dhamma, not force. The first king, Mahāsammata, was elected and called rājā because he “pleases others by Dhamma” (Aggañña Sutta). The Jātakas list ten duties for every righteous ruler.

Body

  1. Dāna (generosity: use wealth for the people) · Sīla (morality: be an example) · Pariccāga (self-sacrifice for the people).
  2. Ajjava (honesty, no corruption) · Maddava (gentleness, approachable) · Tapa (self-control, simple living).
  3. Akkodha (free from anger) · Avihiṃsā (non-violence, peace not war) · Khanti (patience, tolerance) · Avirodhana (not opposing the people's will; following the law).
  4. Supporting teachings: the Universal Monarch protects all and gives to the poor (Cakkavatti-sīhanāda); crime falls with livelihoods, not punishment (Kūṭadanta); avoid the four biases (desire, hatred, delusion, fear).
  5. Example: Asoka governed by Dhamma after Kalinga: hospitals, wells, tolerance, Dhamma officers.
  6. Today: welfare, integrity, accountability, non-violence: modern good governance.

Conclusion

A ruler with the ten duties is generous, moral, self-sacrificing, honest, gentle, disciplined, calm, non-violent, patient and with the people, and creates a just, peaceful society.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Buddhist ethics (sīla) is voluntary training based on reason, compassion and kamma, not commandments. Though the first step to Nibbāna, it is also a social ethic: almost every precept is about how we treat others.

Body

  1. What each precept protects: life and security; property and trust; the family; truth and communication; health, safety and a clear mind.
  2. Responsibility, not law: “I undertake the training rule…”; no punishment; those who fail can start again (Aṅgulimāla).
  3. The golden rule: “What is not pleasing to me is not pleasing to others” (Veḷudvāreyya Sutta): universal, so anyone can accept it.
  4. If people kept them: less violence and terrorism, theft and corruption, family breakdown, fraud and fake news, drug crime and drunk driving: “freedom from fear to countless beings” (AN 8.39).
  5. Self and society: “Protecting oneself, one protects others” (Sedaka Sutta).
  6. Add one or two: the four ways of showing favour (generosity, kind speech, helpful action, equal treatment); Sigālovāda duties; moral shame and fear.

Conclusion

The five precepts are simple, universal and practical. By restraining ourselves from harm we protect life, property, families, truth and clear thinking, and society becomes safe and peaceful.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Gender equality means equal status and opportunity in family, education, work, politics and religion. In ancient India women couldn't study the Vedas or perform rituals: “a woman is never independent.” Against this, the Buddha's approach was reformative and, for its time, revolutionary.

Body

  1. Context first: 6th-century BCE society had fixed roles for men and women (as some villages still do). The Buddha's hesitation and the nuns' extra rules must be seen in that society, partly to protect the nuns.
  2. Equal spiritual potential: women can attain arahantship. The nun Somā answered Māra: “What does womanhood matter when the mind is concentrated?” (SN 5.2). Not-self means gender is secondary.
  3. The Bhikkhunī Order: founded at Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī's request, giving women religious life, education and independence.
  4. Women given authority: Dhammadinnā, foremost Dhamma teacher (her discourse is in the Tipiṭaka); Khemā and Uppalavaṇṇā, chief female disciples; the Therīgāthā.
  5. Lay women: Visākhā, chief female supporter; Ambapālī the courtesan, whose invitation he accepted over the princes'.
  6. In the family: “A daughter may be better than a son” (SN 3.16, to King Pasenadi); reciprocal duties between spouses.

Conclusion

Seen in its context, the Buddha's approach was revolutionary: equal spiritual potential, the Order of nuns, praise for women teachers and supporters, and respect for women in the family.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Every society has disadvantaged people: the poor, sick, elderly, low-caste and marginalised. Buddhism teaches all are equal under the same law of cause and effect, and recognises social causes of poverty, not only individual kamma.

Body

  1. Social causes, state responsibility: in the Cakkavatti-sīhanāda Sutta a king's failure to help the poor led to theft, violence and decline; the Kūṭadanta Sutta says crime falls when people get seed, capital and wages.
  2. Against discrimination: “Not by birth is one a brahmin; by action” (Vāseṭṭha Sutta). Upāli the barber and Sunīta the scavenger were ordained; Ambedkarite Buddhists fight caste today.
  3. The Buddha's example: he washed the neglected sick monk Pūtigatta Tissa, comforted Kisā Gotamī and gave Aṅgulimāla a second chance.
  4. Teachings for helping: the four ways of showing favour; dāna, sīla and bhāvanā; service to others as a meritorious deed; mettā and karuṇā as motivation.
  5. Helping people help themselves: diligence, saving and good friends (Vyagghapajja); avoiding drink, gambling and bad company (Sigālovāda).
  6. Examples: Anāthapiṇḍika, “feeder of the helpless”; Asoka's hospitals and wells; Buddhist clinics, schools and food banks today.

Conclusion

Buddhism supports the disadvantaged by naming the social causes of poverty, making rulers responsible, rejecting discrimination and encouraging generosity and service, as the Buddha himself did.

Memory trick: the two arrows“Pain is the 1st arrow; panic is the 2nd”

The event is the first arrow; worry and despair are the self-made second. Mindfulness stops the second.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Stress, anxiety and depression are widespread today. Buddhism has always placed the mind at the centre of health: the Buddha is “the great physician”. Health is harmony of body, mind and environment, and mindfulness is the main tool.

Body

  1. Mind and body together: two kinds of illness, bodily and mental; rare is anyone free of mental illness even for a moment (Roga Sutta). “Though your body is sick, let your mind not be sick” (to Nakulapitā).
  2. What mindfulness is: present-moment, non-judging awareness of body, feelings, mind and mental objects: “the direct path… for overcoming sorrow” (Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta).
  3. Breathing: the Ānāpānasati Sutta: mindful breathing calms the body and settles anxiety.
  4. Two arrows: feel the pain without adding worry and despair (Salla Sutta).
  5. Thoughts and lifestyle: wholesome thoughts → well-being (Dvedhāvitakka); mettā reduces anger; the Sigālovāda Sutta as a layperson's health code (no intoxicants, balanced sleep and work).
  6. Modern: MBSR and MBCT help anxiety, depression and chronic pain; CBT shares the thought–feeling link.

Conclusion

Mindfulness of body, breath, feelings and thoughts, not firing the second arrow, and balanced, kind living reduce stress and suffering, and modern psychology confirms it.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Buddhism doesn't reject wealth for lay people: poverty is suffering for a householder. The Buddha taught how to earn, protect, use and enjoy wealth righteously, on the Middle Path between greed and deprivation.

Body

  1. Vyagghapajja Sutta: welfare now through diligence, protecting wealth, good friends and balanced living; avoid the four leaks (womanising, drinking, gambling, bad friends); welfare hereafter through faith, virtue, generosity and wisdom.
  2. Sigālovāda Sutta: gather wealth like a bee gathers nectar; split income into four parts; avoid the six ways of wasting it.
  3. Parābhava Sutta: downfall through laziness, not supporting aged parents, drinking and gambling, and greed beyond one's means.
  4. Right livelihood: earn honestly; avoid trading weapons, people, meat, intoxicants and poison.
  5. Using wealth: for family, workers and friends, protection, taxes and offerings, supporting good causes; the four householder happinesses: owning, enjoying, being debt-free and, highest, being blameless.
  6. The state: poverty causes crime, so rulers must provide livelihoods (Kūṭadanta, Cakkavatti).

Conclusion

Buddhist economics encourages work, saving and honest livelihood while keeping wealth under morality, generosity and wisdom, bringing happiness now and support for spiritual progress.

Memory trick: the answer formula“Root first, then remedy”

Start with greed, hatred and delusion as the cause. Then give one or two teachings as the solution.

Memory trick: the peacemaker“Water or blood?”

At the Rohiṇī river the Buddha asked the Sakyas and Koliyas which was worth more. Both laid down their weapons.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Society faces wars, riots, political division and racial and religious tension. Buddhism first looks for the root: every conflict rests on greed, hatred and delusion. Once the cause is clear, Buddhist teachings offer practical solutions.

Body

  1. Root causes: greed for land, wealth and power; hatred, revenge and prejudice; delusion and clinging to “my group is right”. Mental proliferation leads to “taking up weapons” (Madhupiṇḍika Sutta).
  2. Non-hatred: “Hatred is never appeased by hatred; by non-hatred alone” (Dhp 5); mettā breaks the cycle of revenge.
  3. Seven conditions of welfare (Mahāparinibbāna Sutta): meet often and in harmony, respect laws and elders, protect women, shrines and holy people: a model of dialogue and unity.
  4. Four ways of showing favour: generosity, kind speech, helpful action and equal treatment build trust between groups.
  5. Precepts and the Middle Path: remove violence and distrust; avoid extremes.
  6. The Buddha as peacemaker: the Rohiṇī river: “Which is more valuable, water or the blood of warriors?” Rulers following the ten duties prevent conflict.

Conclusion

Conflict is rooted in greed, hatred and delusion. Buddhism resolves it at the root through non-hatred, harmony and dialogue, generosity and equal treatment, the precepts and the Middle Path.

Q2 · the ideal“Live like the bee and the flower”Take the nectar without harming the flower's colour or scent (Dhp 49).
Q3 · which direction“East = where life rises; West = where the day settles”The East (sunrise) is parents, where life begins. The West (sunset) is your spouse, who you settle down with.
Q3 · parents' five duties“Stop · Start · Skill · Spouse · Share”Stop them from evil · start them on good · teach a skill · arrange a spouse · share the inheritance.
Q4 · the ten in order“Dear Sir, Please Act Mildly, Take Anger Away; Keep Agreeing”Dāna · Sīla · Pariccāga · Ajjava · Maddava · Tapa · Akkodha · Avihiṃsā · Khanti · Avirodhana.
Q4 · the first three“Give · Be good · Give up”Dāna (give wealth), sīla (be moral), pariccāga (give up your own comfort for the people).
Q5 · what they protect“LP-FTM”Life · Property · Family · Truth · Mind.
Q5 · the five“Kind Souls Seldom Lie Drunk”Killing · Stealing · Sexual misconduct · Lying · Drink and drugs.
Q6 · the argument“Context, Capacity, Community, Credit”Context (6th c. BCE) · Capacity (equal liberation) · Community (the Bhikkhunī Order) · Credit (praised nuns and lay women).
Q6 · the women to name“Dhamma-teacher, Wise, Wealthy, Welcomed”Dhammadinnā (teacher) · Khemā (wisdom) · Visākhā (supporter) · Ambapālī (welcomed despite her status).
Q7 · the four ways of showing favour“Give, Speak kindly, Help, Treat equally”Dāna · piya-vācā · attha-cariyā · samānattatā (saṅgahavatthu).
Q7 · the Buddha's example“Whoever would care for me should care for the sick”He washed the neglected monk Pūtigatta Tissa with his own hands.
Q8 · the four foundations“Big Feelings Mess Minds”Body · Feelings · Mind · Mental objects.
Q8 · the two arrows“Pain is the 1st arrow; panic is the 2nd”The event is the first arrow; worry and despair are the self-made second. Mindfulness stops the second.
Q9 · present welfare“Work, Watch, Win friends, Weigh spending”Diligence · protecting wealth · good friends · balanced living (Vyagghapajja).
Q9 · the four-part budget“1 to live, 2 to grow, 1 to save”One part daily needs, two parts business, one part saved for emergencies (Sigālovāda).
Q10 · the answer formula“Root first, then remedy”Start with greed, hatred and delusion as the cause. Then give one or two teachings as the solution.
Q10 · the peacemaker“Water or blood?”At the Rohiṇī river the Buddha asked the Sakyas and Koliyas which was worth more. Both laid down their weapons.
