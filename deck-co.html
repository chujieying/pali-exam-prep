Question 1The Four Noble Truths as a framework for counselling

Principal's cue

Memory trick: the four stages“Doctors Always Prescribe Treatment”

Diagnosis · Aetiology · Prognosis · Treatment = the four truths in order.

Memory trick: prognosis“Not no pain: no clinging”

Cessation means the absence of clinging, not the absence of every unpleasant experience.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The Buddha is “the great physician”: identify the illness, its cause, confirm a cure, prescribe treatment. In counselling, conceptualisation organises a client's story into an explanation that guides treatment, and the Four Noble Truths give a complete Buddhist framework for it.

Body

  1. Dukkha = diagnosis: the presenting problem: life events (loss, illness), raw pain and the elaborated reactions (sorrow, despair), and clinging.
  2. Samudaya = aetiology: the underlying ignorance, craving and aversion (e.g. craving for an identity, aversion to the present).
  3. Nirodha = prognosis: relief is possible; cessation is “the absence of clinging”, not of all pain.
  4. Magga = treatment: a plan from the Eightfold Path across environment, body, speech and mind.
  5. Why it's strong: verifiable in the client's experience, explains causes, leads to action, gives hope, simple; underlies MBSR and MBCT.
  6. Example: a 45-year-old loses his job: loss and despair (diagnosis); craving for status, aversion, ignoring impermanence (aetiology); he can meet it with equanimity (prognosis); mindfulness, impermanence reflection, values-based action, family support (treatment).

Conclusion

The Four Noble Truths are a practical clinical framework: diagnostic, causal, hopeful and prescriptive. They let a counsellor understand any client's suffering and guide them to relief.

Memory trick: the four domains“Every Body Speaks Mind”

Memory trick: the six features“Like Very Patient, Healthy, Intelligent Gardeners”

Answer skeleton

Introduction

In the medical model the Eightfold Path is the treatment: liberation-oriented, self-verifiable, present-centred, holistic, integrative and gradual. Each factor can become a practical intervention for any client.

Body

  1. Wisdom factors (understanding): right view = psychoeducation (how suffering arises; thoughts are passing events); right intention = motivation, goodwill and self-compassion instead of self-criticism.
  2. Ethical factors (behaviour): right speech = honest, kind communication; right action = stopping harmful habits, building healthy routines; right livelihood = ethical work and balance.
  3. Mental factors (training): right effort = the four efforts (prevent, abandon, develop, maintain), like CBT; right mindfulness = breath and body practices (MBSR/MBCT); right concentration = calming practice.
  4. Four domains: environment, physical, verbal, mental. For constant anger: avoid triggers; pause and relax; no harsh words; observe the anger and cultivate patience.
  5. Gradual and integrative: ethics steadies the mind, a steady mind supports insight, insight improves behaviour.

Conclusion

The Eightfold Path gives a complete treatment plan, changing understanding, behaviour and the mind together. Practised step by step, it reduces suffering now and points to lasting freedom.

Memory trick: the cognitive spiral“Contact → Feel → Perceive → Think → Spiral”

Proliferation (papañca) is where suffering builds up. Catch it at “feel”.

Memory trick: the three cravings“Have · Be · Not-be”

Craving to have (sense pleasures), to be (existence), not to be (non-existence).

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Buddhist psychology is a system for understanding the mind's nature, potential and problems. The mind isn't a soul but “a stream of momentary events, arising from conditions”, “luminous, but defiled by passing defilements” (AN 1.51).

Body

  1. Five aggregates: a person is form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness; clinging to them as “me” is suffering, so thoughts and feelings are processes, not a fixed self.
  2. Process of perception (MN 18): contact → feeling → perception → thinking → proliferation. E.g. a phone buzz → unpleasant → “another message” → “I'm behind” → “my future is hopeless”. Mindfulness at feeling stops it.
  3. Three roots: greed, hatred and delusion cause distress; their opposites bring well-being.
  4. Three cravings: sensual (addiction, shopping), existence (perfectionism, workaholism), non-existence (avoidance, numbing). Each projects into the future, is charged with pleasure, and drives restless seeking.
  5. Two arrows: pain is the first arrow; worry and despair the second. The trained mind feels only the first.
  6. Mindfulness: attention on purpose, present, non-judging. It changes how clients relate to thoughts, not what they think.

Conclusion

These concepts map how suffering builds in the mind and show where clients can step in, above all through mindfulness, to reduce distress.

Memory trick: the communication suttas“Listen (Dūteyya), Speak well (Vācā), Steer (Kesi)”

Dūteyya: listen and understand first · Vācā: timely, true, gentle, helpful, kind · Kesi: gentle, firm, both, or end and refer.

Memory trick: balance the sublime qualities“Warmth needs balance”

Compassion without equanimity → fatigue; equanimity without loving-kindness → cold detachment.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

A Buddhist counsellor uses basic doctrines both to understand the client and to shape the relationship itself, without imposing Buddhism.

Body

  1. Four Truths and Eightfold Path as framework and treatment.
  2. Three characteristics: impermanence (“this feeling will pass”), dukkha (suffering is part of life, not failure), not-self (loosens rigid identities like “I am a failure”).
  3. Kamma: intentional action gives clients responsibility and the power to change; not everything is past kamma.
  4. Four sublime qualities in the counsellor: loving-kindness (safety), compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity (stability, no burnout), kept in balance.
  5. Three purities (code of ethics): body (no harm or exploitation, confidentiality), speech (no false reassurance, no harsh or idle talk), mind (no longing for validation, no ill will, right view).
  6. Communication: Dūteyya (listen first), Vācā (right time, true, gentle, beneficial, kind), Kesi (gentle, firm, both, or end and refer). Plus practices: breath, body scan, loving-kindness.

Conclusion

These doctrines give the counsellor a way to understand the client and a way to be with the client. Used sensitively, they make the relationship itself healing.

Question 5Case study: applying Buddhist counselling to a client

Principal's cue

Counselling area 5: “case-study to do.” Expect a short case to analyse. Learn the structure and practise it on anxiety, grief, anger, addiction, burnout.

Case studyMei, 30: layoffs, anxiety
1 · Relationship
  • Safe space, listen fully
  • Validate: “It makes sense”
2 · Composure
  • Slow breath, long out-breath
  • Feet on the floor
3 · Diagnosis
  • 1st arrow: job uncertainty
  • 2nd: “my life is over”, no sleep
4 · Aetiology
  • Craving to be (bhava-taṇhā)
  • Aversion to uncertainty
  • Restless email-checking
5 · Prognosis
  • Can't control layoffs
  • Can stop the 2nd arrow
6 · Treatment & limits
  • Mental, physical, verbal, environment
  • Equanimity; refer if severe
Memory trick: the structure“Relate, Calm, Diagnose, Dig, Hope, Treat”

Relationship → composure → diagnosis → aetiology → prognosis → treatment (and refer if needed).

Memory trick: the key move“Name it to tame it”

“This is a worried thought.” Naming creates distance from the thought.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Case: Mei, 30, has been very anxious since her company announced layoffs. She can't sleep, checks her email constantly and says, “If I lose this job, my life is over.”

Body

  1. Relationship: a safe, quiet space; listen fully without rushing to advise (Dūteyya); validate gently (Vācā): “It makes sense to feel this way.”
  2. Immediate composure: slow breathing with a longer out-breath (“calming the bodily formation”, Ānāpānasati) and grounding (feet, sounds).
  3. Diagnosis: first arrow: job uncertainty. Second arrow: catastrophic thinking, sleeplessness, compulsive checking.
  4. Aetiology: craving for existence (clinging to her identity as a successful employee); aversion to uncertainty projected into the future; ignoring impermanence. Email-checking is the “restless seeking” phase.
  5. Prognosis: she can't control the layoffs, but she can stop firing the second arrow and meet uncertainty more calmly.
  6. Treatment: mental (name the worry, impermanence, “May I be at ease”); physical (daily breath practice, sleep routine, exercise); verbal (supportive friends); environment (email at set times, update her CV). Counsellor holds compassion with equanimity and refers if it becomes severe.

Conclusion

With the Four Noble Truths as the framework, Mei sees that most of her suffering is the second arrow, driven by craving and aversion. Mindfulness, self-compassion and practical action reduce her anxiety even though the situation stays uncertain.
