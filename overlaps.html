Model answer 1The five precepts: meaning and benefits

EB Q3SD Q5PL Q9

Introduction

The five precepts (pañca-sīla) are the basic ethical training of every Buddhist lay person. They are chanted as "Pāṇātipātā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi…", "I undertake the training rule to abstain from…". The key words are sikkhāpada (training rule) and samādiyāmi (I undertake). So the precepts are not commandments from a god or laws enforced by punishment. They are a voluntary moral and ethical responsibility that each person takes on.

Body

PāliAbstain from…Protects
Pāṇātipātākilling living beingslife
Adinnādānātaking what is not givenproperty
Kāmesu micchācārāsexual misconductfamily, trust
Musāvādāfalse speechtruth
Surā-meraya-majja-pamādaṭṭhānāintoxicants causing heedlessnessa clear mind, health, safety
  1. An ethical responsibility, not rules. In many religions a follower must obey rules and can be rejected for breaking them: Jains must be strict vegetarians; Muslims must not drink alcohol. Buddhism has no such enforcement. If someone breaks a precept, they are not thrown out. They can recognise the mistake, correct themselves and take the precepts again. Aṅgulimāla had killed many people, yet the Buddha accepted him and he became an arahant.
  2. Self-restraint from harm. At heart the precepts mean restraining oneself from harmful actions. Anyone can see these acts are harmful, so the precepts don't depend on being a Buddhist. The Buddha's reasoning: "I want to live and don't want to die… what is not pleasing to me is not pleasing to others" (Veḷudvāreyya Sutta). "All fear the rod, all love life" (Dhp 130).
  3. Intention decides. Accidentally stepping on an insect is not breaking the first precept. Moving someone's ten cents with the intention of taking it is already stealing.
  4. Benefits for oneself. A clear conscience and freedom from fear and remorse; a good reputation and trust; health and wealth protected (especially by the 5th precept); a calm mind, the foundation for meditation; dying without confusion and a good rebirth.
  5. Benefits for others. Others are safe from violence, theft, betrayal, lies and drunken behaviour. The Buddha called the precepts "great gifts" that give "freedom from fear, hostility and oppression to countless beings" (AN 8.39).

Conclusion

The five precepts are voluntary training rules: a personal responsibility to restrain oneself from harming others. A person who fails can always correct themselves and begin again. Because the precepts rest on universal reasoning, they benefit both the person who keeps them and everyone around them, Buddhist or not.

Early Buddhism Q3: stress

"Not rules but ethical responsibility", the comparison with other religions, Aṅgulimāla, intention, and benefits for you and for others. Those are the principal's exact cues.

Social Dimensions Q5: add
  • What each precept protects in society (Life, Property, Family, Truth, Mind) and what society would look like if people kept them: less violence and terrorism, theft and corruption, broken families, fraud and fake news, drug crime and drunk driving.
  • "Protecting oneself, one protects others" (Sedaka Sutta, SN 47.19).
  • One or two other social ethics: the four ways of showing favour (generosity, kind speech, helpful action, equal treatment) or Sigālovāda Sutta duties.
Pāli Q9: write the Pāli
  • All five in full: Pāṇātipātā / Adinnādānā / Kāmesu micchācārā / Musāvādā / Surāmerayamajjapamādaṭṭhānā veramaṇī sikkhāpadaṃ samādiyāmi.
  • Eight precepts: 3rd becomes Abrahmacariyā; add Vikālabhojanā, Nacca-gīta-vādita-visūkadassana-mālā-gandha-vilepana-dhāraṇa-maṇḍana-vibhūsanaṭṭhānā, Uccāsayana-mahāsayanā.
  • Glosses: veramaṇī = abstention · sikkhāpadaṃ = training rule · samādiyāmi = I undertake.

Model answer 2Kamma, intention and the ten wholesome & unwholesome deeds

EB Q4EB Q5CO Q4

Introduction

In modern usage "karma" often means bad luck or punishment. In Buddhism, kamma simply means action, good or bad, and more precisely intentional action. The Buddha defined it: "Cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi. Cetayitvā kammaṃ karoti kāyena vācāya manasā": "Monks, it is intention that I call kamma. Having intended, one acts through body, speech and mind" (Nibbedhika Sutta, AN 6.63). The Buddha summarised moral action as the ten wholesome and unwholesome courses of action, grouped by these three doors.

Body

  1. Intention is the essence of kamma. Without intention there is no kamma. Stepping on an ant by accident is not the kamma of killing. But even a tiny act with a wrong intention is kamma: moving someone's ten-cent coin a short way, intending to take it, is already stealing.
  2. The ten unwholesome deeds (3 – 4 – 3).
    DoorUnwholesomeWholesome
    Body (3)killing · stealing · sexual misconductprotecting life · generosity · faithfulness
    Speech (4)lying · slander · harsh speech · idle chattertruth · speech that unites · kind words · meaningful talk
    Mind (3)covetousness · ill will · wrong viewcontentment (no greed) · loving-kindness (no anger) · right view
    The bodily three are the first three precepts and lying is the fourth; the Buddha added three more kinds of harmful speech and three mental actions, because "mind is the forerunner of all things" (Dhp 1).
  3. Roots and results. Unwholesome deeds come from greed, hatred and delusion and bring suffering and unhappy rebirths. Wholesome deeds come from generosity, loving-kindness and wisdom and bring happiness, trust and a calm mind.
  4. The difference from Jainism. Jainism, the religion closest to Buddhism in the Buddha's time, also taught kamma. But for the Jains the bodily act counted most, even if unintentional. That is why Jain monks sweep the path and filter their water. The Buddha rejected this: intention counts most. In the Upāli Sutta (MN 56) he showed that mental action is the most powerful.
  5. Kamma is not fate. Because kamma is intentional, we are responsible for our actions and free to change them. Not everything is due to past kamma; illness, climate and carelessness also play a part (Sīvaka Sutta). Kamma is a natural law of cause and effect, not reward or punishment from a god.

Conclusion

Kamma means intentional action, good or bad, not luck or fate. "It is intention that I call kamma." This sets the Buddhist teaching apart, especially from Jainism. The ten wholesome and unwholesome deeds (three by body, four by speech, three by mind) show that Buddhist ethics covers what we do, say and think, and that by purifying our intentions we shape our own future.

Early Buddhism: two separate questions
  • Q4 (ten deeds): lead with the table and the body/speech/mind categories; keep kamma short.
  • Q5 (kamma): lead with the quote, intention, the 10-cent example and Jainism; use the ten deeds as one paragraph. The principal starred intention, so make it the centre.
Counselling Q4: turn it into practice
  • Responsibility: because kamma is intention, clients can change their future through present choices, without blame or fatalism (not everything is past kamma).
  • Three purities (the counsellor's code of ethics, built on the ten wholesome deeds): body (no harm or exploitation, confidentiality), speech (no false reassurance, no harsh or shaming words, no idle chatter), mind (no longing for validation, no ill will, right view).

Model answer 3The Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path

EB Q7EB Q8CO Q1CO Q2

Introduction

The Four Noble Truths were the subject of the Buddha's first sermon, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, and are the heart of his teaching. They follow a doctor's method: the illness (suffering), its cause (craving), the cure (cessation) and the medicine (the path). The fourth truth, the Noble Eightfold Path, is the Middle Way between sensual indulgence and self-torment.

Body

  1. Suffering (dukkha), to be understood. Birth, ageing, sickness and death; sorrow and grief; being with what we dislike, separated from what we love, not getting what we want; in short, the five aggregates of clinging.
  2. The cause (samudaya), to be abandoned. Craving (taṇhā) of three kinds: for sense pleasures, for existence and for non-existence.
  3. Cessation (nirodha), to be realised. When craving ends, suffering ends. This is Nibbāna. It proves freedom is possible.
  4. The path (magga), to be developed. The eight factors in three groups:
    GroupFactorsIn daily life
    Wisdomright view · right intentionaccept change, take responsibility; goodwill instead of anger
    Moralityright speech · right action · right livelihoodtrust at home and work; clear conscience; honest income
    Concentrationright effort · right mindfulness · right concentrationgood habits; less stress, respond not react; inner calm
  5. The path's purpose. Its final purpose is enlightenment. But until we reach that goal we still have to live, so the path is also a practical guide for a peaceful and happy life now. The factors are developed together, like the strands of a rope.
  6. Why "noble" (ariya)? Most of all, because they are true at any time, for anyone, in any place. Many truths are relative: something is "white" only next to something darker; someone is "tall" only next to someone shorter. The Four Noble Truths are true in America and Japan, in the Buddha's time and today. Also: they were taught by the Noble One, and realising them makes a person noble.
  7. Realistic, not pessimistic. Starting with suffering is like a doctor starting with a diagnosis. The teaching goes on to the cause, the cure and the method.

Conclusion

The Four Noble Truths explain suffering, its cause, its end and the way to its end. They are noble because they hold for anyone, any time, anywhere. The Eightfold Path, as wisdom, morality and concentration, leads finally to Nibbāna, and along the way gives a peaceful, happy life in the present.

Early Buddhism: two separate questions
  • Q8 (Four Truths): the four truths with their tasks, then a full paragraph on why noble (white / tall example). That paragraph is the principal's ☐ box.
  • Q7 (Path): the eight factors in the three groups, then the "peaceful, happy life now" paragraph with daily-life examples. The principal said: explain what kind of peaceful life each gives.
Counselling Q1: the Four Truths as a framework
  • Rename the truths: Diagnosis (the presenting problem: first arrow + second arrow), Aetiology (which craving, aversion, ignorance), Prognosis ("the absence of clinging", not of all pain), Treatment.
  • Why it's a strong framework: verifiable in the client's experience, explains causes, leads to action, gives hope, simple; basis of MBSR/MBCT.
  • Add a short case (e.g. a man who lost his job: clinging to "I am a manager").
Counselling Q2: the Path as treatment
  • Each factor as an intervention: right view = psychoeducation; right intention = motivation, self-compassion; speech / action / livelihood = communication skills, behaviour change, work-life balance; right effort = the four efforts (like CBT); mindfulness = MBSR/MBCT; concentration = calming practice.
  • The four domains: environment, physical, verbal, mental. Example for anger: avoid triggers · pause and relax · no harsh words · observe anger, cultivate patience.

Model answer 4The five aggregates: the Buddhist analysis of the person

EB Q9CO Q3

Introduction

Buddhism teaches that there is no permanent self (anattā). Yet we are clearly living beings, so how does Buddhism explain the individual? The Buddha analysed the person into five groups or aggregates (pañcakkhandha). What we call "I" is only a combination of these five changing processes. This is the Buddhist explanation of empirical existence.

Body

  1. The five aggregates.
    AggregateMeaningSimile (SN 22.95)
    Form (rūpa)the body: earth (solidity), water (cohesion), fire (heat), air (motion)a lump of foam on a river: looks solid, hollow inside
    Feeling (vedanā)pleasant, painful or neutrala bubble on water: comes and goes fast
    Perception (saññā)recognising, labellinga mirage: water far away, none up close
    Mental formations (saṅkhāra)intentions, thoughts, emotions, habits; kamma is made herea banana trunk: peel it, there's no core
    Consciousness (viññāṇa)knowing through the six sensesa magician's trick: seems real, isn't
    Form is the material side (rūpa); the other four are the mental side (nāma): together, nāmarūpa.
  2. The aggregates of clinging. When we cling to them as "this is mine, this is I, this is my self", they become the five aggregates of clinging, which the First Noble Truth calls suffering.
  3. No self in them. Each aggregate changes and can't be controlled. If the body were the self, we could say "let my body be like this" (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta). Just as "chariot" is a name for assembled parts, "being" is a name for the aggregates together (Vajirā Sutta).
  4. Why it matters. Seeing the person as five changing processes reduces selfishness, pride and clinging, and is the basis of insight meditation.

Conclusion

Buddhism explains the individual as five changing aggregates, not a permanent self. The similes of foam, bubble, mirage, banana trunk and magic trick show that each seems solid but has no core. Understanding this reduces clinging and leads to liberation.

Early Buddhism Q9: keep it at "surface level"

The principal said a surface understanding is enough: name the five, explain each in a line, give the similes, link to not-self. Mention the Abhidhamma's 28 kinds of matter only in passing, if at all.

Counselling Q3: add the psychology
  • Process of perception (Madhupiṇḍika Sutta, MN 18): sense + object + consciousness = contact → feeling → perception → thinking → proliferation (papañca). Example: a phone buzz → unpleasant feeling → "another message" → "I'm falling behind" → "my future is hopeless".
  • Mindfulness at the feeling step stops the spiral.
  • Other concepts to list: the three roots, the three types of craving, the two arrows (Salla Sutta), the mind as "luminous but defiled by passing defilements" (AN 1.51).
  • For clients: thoughts and feelings are events, not facts and not "me".

Model answer 5The Second Council and the first split in the Saṅgha

GE Q2EB Q1

Introduction

Before he passed away, the Buddha appointed no successor: "The Dhamma and Vinaya will be your teacher." To protect them, the Saṅgha held councils. The First Council (three months after the Parinibbāna, at Rājagaha) recited the Dhamma and Vinaya. About 100 years later, a dispute over discipline led to the Second Council, which protected the Vinaya but also saw the first division of the Saṅgha.

Body

  1. Cause. The Vajjian monks of Vesālī practised ten points that broke the Vinaya, the most serious being accepting gold and silver (money). Ven. Yasa Kākaṇḍakaputta protested, was punished for it, and sought the support of senior monks: Sambhūta, Revata and Sabbakāmī, the most senior monk alive.
  2. Details. Held at the Vālukārāma in Vesālī, about 100 years after the Parinibbāna, under King Kālāsoka, with 700 arahants.
  3. Method. To avoid endless argument, a committee of eight monks, four from the east and four from the west, examined the points. Revata asked the questions and Sabbakāmī answered. This shows a democratic way of settling disputes.
  4. Outcome. All ten points were rejected as against the Vinaya, and the Dhamma and Vinaya were recited again. The rule against handling money was protected.
  5. The first split. The Saṅgha divided into two groups: the Sthaviras (elders), who accepted the elders' decision and wanted to keep the tradition as it was, and the Mahāsāṅghikas ("great assembly", the larger group), who wanted change and to make Buddhism more popular. Over time they divided into about 18 schools.
  6. Not Theravāda vs Mahāyāna. This is a common misunderstanding. The terms Mahāyāna ("great vehicle") and Hīnayāna came much later, when some taught the aim of becoming a Buddha. The elders' tradition later called itself Theravāda, "the teaching of the elders". This developed gradually, not at one council.
  7. Rituals began after it. After the Second Council, ritual practices (offering lights and flowers, chanting, worshipping images) were gradually introduced. Before it, there were no such practices.

Conclusion

The Second Council protected the purity of the Vinaya and showed a fair way of settling disputes, but it also marked the first division of the Saṅgha into elders and Mahāsāṅghikas. Theravāda's strict, conservative character comes from these elders. The later Theravāda/Mahāyāna division should not be confused with this split.

Model answer 6Mindfulness and meditation: from calm to mental health

EB Q2SD Q8CO

Introduction

Meditation (bhāvanā) means "mental cultivation". Meditation is not unique to Buddhism; in the Buddha's time almost every tradition had its own. What makes Buddhist meditation distinctive is that it combines samatha (calm) with vipassanā (insight), and aims at wisdom and freedom from suffering. Running through both is mindfulness (sati), present-moment awareness, which today is also a major tool for mental health.

Body

  1. Samatha: calm and concentration. The mind rests on one object (the breath, loving-kindness) until it becomes calm and one-pointed, suppressing the five hindrances. Samatha was not introduced by the Buddha: before his enlightenment he practised it under Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta and reached high attainments, but saw they were temporary and did not end suffering.
  2. Vipassanā: insight. "Seeing clearly". This is what the Buddha himself discovered and taught. The meditator observes body, feelings, mind and mental objects (the four foundations, Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta, MN 10) and sees the three characteristics: impermanence, suffering and not-self. This removes ignorance and craving and leads to Nibbāna.
  3. Supporting elements. Right effort; morality as the foundation (a clear conscience makes concentration easier); and both meditations working together, calm giving stability and insight giving wisdom.
  4. Mind and body are one system. The Roga Sutta (AN 4.157) speaks of two illnesses, bodily and mental; people may be free of bodily illness for years, but rarely free of mental illness even for a moment. The Buddha told the sick old Nakulapitā: "Though your body is sick, let your mind not be sick" (SN 22.1).
  5. Mindfulness for mental health.
    • Breath: the Ānāpānasati Sutta (MN 118), "calming the bodily formation, I breathe in". It slows the body and settles anxiety.
    • The two arrows: in the Salla Sutta (SN 36.6) pain is the first arrow; worry, resistance and despair are a self-made second arrow. Mindfulness lets us feel the first without firing the second.
    • Thoughts and emotions: observing them without attachment prevents chronic stress (Dvedhāvitakka Sutta, MN 19); loving-kindness reduces anger.
  6. Modern applications. Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR, Jon Kabat-Zinn, 1979) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT, for depression relapse) show measurable benefits for anxiety, depression and chronic pain. CBT shares Buddhism's focus on the link between thoughts, feelings and behaviour.

Conclusion

Buddhist meditation joins samatha, which calms the mind and existed before the Buddha, with vipassanā, the Buddha's own insight into impermanence, suffering and not-self. Mindfulness links them, and its value for mental health, from the two arrows to MBSR, is now confirmed by modern psychology.

Early Buddhism Q2: key elements of meditation

Use points 1–3 fully. The principal's cues: "meditation is not only for Buddhism", start with the two types, say samatha existed before (the Buddha's earlier teachers), vipassanā = insight into anicca, dukkha, anattā. Keep points 4–6 to a line or skip them.

Social Dimensions Q8: mindfulness & mental health

Lead with points 4–6. Open with the problem (stress, anxiety, depression today) and "the Buddha as the great physician". Add the Sigālovāda Sutta as a "layperson's code of health" (no intoxicants, balanced sleep and work, money and friends) and the Maṅgala Sutta's 38 blessings.

Counselling: as an intervention
  • Kabat-Zinn's definition: "paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, non-judgementally". It changes the client's relationship to thoughts, not their content.
  • Calming an anxious client: calm presence → slow breathing, longer out-breath → grounding (feet, five senses) → name it ("this is anxiety") → separate 1st and 2nd arrow → "May I be at ease" → refer if severe.
