Question 1What is Early Buddhism? Its main features, language and timeline

Principal's cue

Pāli Buddhism is generally called Early Buddhism, but we can't say Theravāda or Mahāyāna is Early Buddhism. Why? Basic doctrine is the same everywhere. Language: Māgadhī; study in your own mother tongue. Timeline: give each view with its reason. The 2nd Council split is NOT Theravāda vs Mahāyāna.

Memory trick: the language point“Māgadhī Mouth, Pāli Later, Sanskrit Script”

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Early Buddhism is the teaching and practice of the Buddha and his first disciples before the later schools. It is “a system of religious practice founded on a philosophical, ethical and pragmatic basis”, aimed at understanding life and reaching liberation by one's own effort.

Body

  1. Not one tradition. Pāli Buddhism preserves the oldest layer, but we can't say Theravāda or Mahāyāna is Early Buddhism, because all traditions share the same basic doctrine (Four Truths, Path, three characteristics, kamma). Only the explanations differ.
  2. Language. The Buddha's mother tongue was Māgadhī, close to Pāli. The name “Pāli” came later in Sri Lanka. He refused to put his teaching into Vedic style and let people learn it in their own language. Sanskrit was later used for writing. So no language proves “earliest”.
  3. Dhamma and Vinaya. “The Dhamma and Vinaya will be your teacher.” Only these were recited at the 1st Council; the Abhidhamma is first clearly mentioned at the 3rd.
  4. Timeline, with reasons. (1) First 20 years: no Vinaya rules, only sincere seekers. (2) Up to the 1st Council: only Dhamma & Vinaya. (3) Up to the 2nd Council: before rituals and the first split. (4) Up to the Theravāda/Mahāyāna division.
  5. The 2nd Council split was elders (Sthaviras) vs Mahāsāṅghikas, not Theravāda vs Mahāyāna.
  6. Main features. Practice rather than ritual (against the costly rituals of 6th-century BCE India); free inquiry; salvation without a saviour; the basic doctrines.

Conclusion

Early Buddhism is the practical, ethical and philosophical teaching preserved in the basic doctrines. No tradition, language or period can be proved “earliest”, but all share the same core: practice over ritual, personal verification, and liberation by one's own effort.

Question 2Key elements of Buddhist meditation: samatha and vipassanā

Principal's cue

Meditation is not only for Buddhism. Start with the two types: samatha (concentration) already existed, so the Buddha practised it under his earlier teachers; vipassanā (insight) is what he really taught, seeing anicca, dukkha, anattā. Explain at the surface.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Meditation (bhāvanā) means mental cultivation. It is not unique to Buddhism: in the Buddha's time most traditions had meditation. Buddhist meditation is distinctive because it combines samatha (calm) and vipassanā (insight) and aims at wisdom and liberation.

Body

  1. Samatha. The mind rests on one object (breath, loving-kindness) until calm and one-pointed; it suppresses the five hindrances and can lead to jhāna.
  2. Samatha was not introduced by the Buddha. As the Bodhisatta he practised it under Āḷāra Kālāma and Uddaka Rāmaputta and reached high states, but saw they were temporary and left them.
  3. Vipassanā. “Seeing clearly”: the meditation the Buddha himself discovered. Observing body, feelings, mind and mental objects (Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta), one sees impermanence, suffering and not-self, which removes ignorance and craving.
  4. Other elements. Mindfulness runs through both; right effort; morality as the base of the threefold training; calm and insight work together.
  5. Benefits. Less stress, better concentration, emotional balance, kinder relationships, and finally wisdom and freedom.

Conclusion

Buddhist meditation joins samatha, which calms the mind and existed before the Buddha, with vipassanā, his own insight into impermanence, suffering and not-self. Supported by morality and mindfulness, they lead from a calm mind to liberation.

Question 3The five precepts: meaning and benefits for oneself and others

Principal's cue

Know the meaning, not just the chant. Precepts are not rules or laws; they are an ethical responsibility. Other religions expel rule-breakers; Buddhism doesn't (Aṅgulimāla). Self-restraint from harm. Benefits for you and for others.

Memory trick: the five“Kind Souls Seldom Lie Drunk”

Killing · Stealing · Sexual misconduct · Lying · Drink and drugs.

Memory trick: not rules“A promise, not a prison”

Precepts are a promise you make to yourself. Break it, and you correct yourself and start again, as Aṅgulimāla did.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The five precepts are every Buddhist lay person's basic ethical training: “I undertake the training rule (sikkhāpada) to abstain from…”. They are a voluntary moral responsibility, not commandments or laws enforced by punishment.

Body

  1. The five: abstaining from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false speech, and intoxicants that cause heedlessness.
  2. Responsibility, not rules. Other religions can reject followers who break their rules (Jain vegetarianism, the Muslim ban on alcohol). Buddhism doesn't expel: you correct yourself and begin again. Aṅgulimāla was accepted and became an arahant.
  3. Self-restraint from harm. “What is not pleasing to me is not pleasing to others” (Veḷudvāreyya Sutta). Any human can see these acts are harmful, so the precepts suit everyone.
  4. Intention decides. Accidentally stepping on an insect isn't killing; moving someone's ten cents to take it is stealing.
  5. Benefits for oneself: clear conscience, no fear or remorse, good reputation, health and wealth protected, a calm mind, a good death and rebirth.
  6. Benefits for others: safety from violence, theft, betrayal, lies, drunkenness: “freedom from fear… to countless beings” (AN 8.39).

Conclusion

The five precepts are a voluntary, personal responsibility to restrain oneself from harm. Anyone who fails can start again. Based on universal reasoning, they benefit both the keeper and everyone around them.

Question 4The ten wholesome and unwholesome actions

Principal's cue

Name all ten. The first four come from the precepts. Then speech, then mind (no greed, no anger…). Categorise into body, speech and mind. Basic meaning only.

Memory trick: the ten“KSS · Little Snakes Hiss Idly · 3 Cs”

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Buddhism divides actions into wholesome (kusala), from non-greed, non-hatred and wisdom, and unwholesome (akusala), from greed, hatred and delusion. The Buddha summarised them as ten courses of action through the three doors of body, speech and mind.

Body

  1. Body (3): killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, the same as the first three precepts.
  2. Speech (4): lying (the 4th precept), plus slander, harsh speech and idle chatter.
  3. Mind (3): covetousness, ill will and wrong view. They count because the mind is the source of all action (Dhp 1).
  4. The wholesome ten: abstaining from these and doing their opposites: protecting life, generosity, faithfulness, truth, uniting and kind speech, meaningful talk, contentment, loving-kindness, right view.
  5. Intention makes all ten kamma; the same act can be wholesome or not depending on it.
  6. Results: unwholesome → suffering and unhappy rebirths; wholesome → happiness, trust, peace of mind and support for the path.

Conclusion

The ten deeds are a complete guide to moral life: three by body, four by speech, three by mind. Buddhist ethics covers what we do, say and think.

Question 5“Intention is kamma”: the Buddhist teaching of kamma

Principal's cue

★ Starred in your notes. Kamma isn't “bad luck”; it means action, good or bad. Most important: intention. Quote “Cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi”. No intention, no kamma. Compare Jainism: body most important (they sweep paths, filter water). The Buddha said intention.

Memory trick: the quote“Chetana-hum: I call kamma intention”

Cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi: “Monks, it is intention that I call kamma.”

Answer skeleton

Introduction

In modern usage “karma” often means bad luck. In Buddhism kamma simply means action, good or bad, and precisely intentional action: “Cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi”, “Monks, it is intention that I call kamma; having intended, one acts by body, speech and mind” (AN 6.63).

Body

  1. Intention is the essence. Without intention, no kamma: stepping on an ant by accident isn't killing. With intention even a tiny act is kamma: moving someone's 10-cent coin to take it is stealing.
  2. Three doors. Intention acts through body, speech and mind: the ten wholesome and unwholesome deeds.
  3. Wholesome and unwholesome. “If one acts with a corrupt mind, suffering follows like the wheel the ox; with a pure mind, happiness follows like a shadow” (Dhp 1–2).
  4. Against Jainism. For the Jains, the bodily act counted most even if unintended, so their monks sweep paths and filter water. The Buddha rejected this; mental action is the most powerful (Upāli Sutta).
  5. Not fate. We are responsible and free to change; not everything is past kamma (Sīvaka Sutta). It's a natural law, not divine reward or punishment.

Conclusion

Kamma is intentional action, good or bad, not luck or fate. Making intention central sets the Buddha's teaching apart from Jainism and lets everyone shape their future by cultivating wholesome intentions.

Memory trick: how they link“Changes → Can't satisfy → Can't be me”

What is impermanent is unsatisfactory; what is impermanent and unsatisfactory is not-self.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The three characteristics, impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha) and not-self (anattā), describe all conditioned things. They are universal: experienced by all beings, everywhere, in ordinary daily life.

Body

  1. Impermanence. “All conditioned things are impermanent” (Dhp 277). Seasons change, bodies age, feelings change by the moment. Why: everything depends on conditions, and when they change, the thing changes.
  2. Dukkha. “All conditioned things are dukkha” (Dhp 278): obvious pain and the unsatisfactoriness of pleasant things. Why: what is impermanent can't give lasting satisfaction; clinging to it hurts when it changes.
  3. Not-self. “All things are not-self” (Dhp 279): no permanent self, only the five aggregates. Why: if the body were self we could say “let it be thus” and control it; we can't (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta).
  4. Connected: impermanent → unsatisfactory → not-self: three views of one reality.
  5. Practical value: accepting change and loss calmly, less selfishness and pride, and the content of insight meditation.

Conclusion

Everything changes because it depends on conditions; what changes can't satisfy; what changes and can't satisfy is not a self. Seeing this in daily life brings peace, and seeing it deeply brings liberation.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The Noble Eightfold Path is the Fourth Noble Truth: the way to the end of suffering, taught as the Middle Way between indulgence and self-torment. Its final purpose is Nibbāna, but it is also a practical guide for a peaceful, happy life here and now.

Body

  1. Wisdom: right view (Four Truths, kamma) and right intention (renunciation, goodwill, harmlessness).
  2. Morality: right speech (no lying, slander, harsh or idle talk), right action (no killing, stealing, misconduct), right livelihood (honest work).
  3. Concentration: right effort (drop bad states, grow good ones), right mindfulness (body, feelings, mind, objects), right concentration (a calm, one-pointed mind).
  4. Final purpose: morality controls actions, concentration calms the mind, wisdom removes ignorance and craving → Nibbāna.
  5. Purpose now: each factor brings peace today: right view → less blaming; right speech → harmony at home and work; right livelihood → no fear or guilt; mindfulness → less stress.
  6. Together: the factors grow together like strands of a rope; it's “noble” because taught by the Noble One and it makes one noble.

Conclusion

Grouped as wisdom, morality and concentration, the Eightfold Path leads to Nibbāna, and along the way every factor is a guide to a peaceful, happy life in the present.

Question 8The Four Noble Truths: why are they called “noble”?

Principal's cue

Dukkha, samudaya, nirodha, magga. ☐ Why noble? True any time, for anyone, in any place. Compare relative truths: “white” beside something whiter, “tall” beside someone taller. True in America, in Japan, then and now. Also: taught by the Noble One.

Memory trick: the four tasks“You Are Really Done”

Answer skeleton

Introduction

The Four Noble Truths were the Buddha's first sermon (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta) and the heart of his teaching. They follow a doctor's method: illness, cause, cure, medicine.

Body

  1. Suffering (dukkha): birth, ageing, sickness, death; sorrow; separation from the loved; not getting what we want; the five aggregates of clinging. To be understood.
  2. Cause (samudaya): craving for sense pleasures, for existence, for non-existence. To be abandoned.
  3. Cessation (nirodha): when craving ends, suffering ends: Nibbāna. To be realised.
  4. Path (magga): the Noble Eightfold Path. To be developed.
  5. Why noble: above all, true at any time, for anyone, anywhere. Relative truths depend on comparison (“white”, “tall”); these don't. Also: taught by the Noble One and realising them makes one noble.
  6. Realistic, not pessimistic: beginning with suffering is like a doctor beginning with a diagnosis.

Conclusion

The Four Noble Truths explain suffering, its cause, its end and the way. They are noble because they are universally true (any time, anyone, anywhere) and because the Noble One taught them.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Buddhism teaches there is no permanent self, yet we are living beings. The Buddha explained the individual as five aggregates (pañcakkhandha): what we call “I” is only these five changing processes together.

Body

  1. Form: the body, made of the four elements (solidity, cohesion, heat, motion). Feeling: pleasant, painful or neutral. Perception: recognising and labelling. Mental formations: intentions, thoughts, emotions, habits, where kamma is made. Consciousness: knowing through the six senses.
  2. Form is the material side (rūpa), the other four the mental side (nāma): together nāmarūpa.
  3. Similes (SN 22.95): form like a lump of foam floating down a river: solid from afar, hollow inside; feeling like a bubble that appears and bursts; perception like a mirage; formations like a banana trunk with no core; consciousness like a magician's trick.
  4. Aggregates of clinging: grasped as “mine, I, my self”, they are what the First Truth calls suffering.
  5. Not-self: each changes and can't be controlled; “being” is just a name for the aggregates together, like “chariot” for its parts.

Conclusion

The individual is five changing aggregates, not a permanent self. The similes show each seems solid but has no core. Seeing this reduces clinging and leads to liberation.

Question 10What is Nibbāna? Its two types

Principal's cue

First explain what Nibbāna is, then the two types: with remainder (body remains, attained in life) and without remainder (body also ends). Basic doctrine, but very little detail in the texts. One page is enough.

Memory trick: the two types“With body, without body”

With remainder: the arahant still has a body (Buddha at 35). Without remainder: at final passing, nothing remains (Buddha at 80).

Memory trick: what it ends“Blow out the three fires: Greed, Hate, Fog”

Greed, hatred and delusion. Nibbāna = “blowing out”.

Answer skeleton

Introduction

Nibbāna is the final goal and the Third Noble Truth: the cessation of suffering. Literally “blowing out”, it is the extinguishing of greed, hatred and delusion (SN 38.1), “the highest happiness” (Dhp 204).

Body

  1. Nature: the complete end of craving, suffering and rebirth; unconditioned (“not born, not become, not made”), so not subject to change.
  2. Not annihilation and not a heaven; it can be realised in this very life. The suttas give little detail and describe it mainly by what it is not, because it is beyond ordinary language.
  3. With remainder (sa-upādisesa): attained during life. Defilements are destroyed but the body and aggregates remain; bodily pain can still be felt, without craving or aversion. The Buddha at Bodhgayā, age 35.
  4. Without remainder (anupādisesa): at the arahant's passing, the aggregates also end, with no rebirth: parinibbāna. The Buddha at Kusinārā, age 80.
  5. Why the distinction matters: Nibbāna's peace can be lived now, as the Buddha and arahants showed.

Conclusion

Nibbāna is the extinguishing of greed, hatred and delusion and the end of suffering: with remainder in life, without remainder at final passing. It is the highest happiness, reachable in this life through the Eightfold Path.

Q2 · what insight sees“I Don't Own it”Impermanence · Dukkha · nOt-self.
Q3 · the five“Kind Souls Seldom Lie Drunk”Killing · Stealing · Sexual misconduct · Lying · Drink and drugs.
Q3 · not rules“A promise, not a prison”Precepts are a promise you make to yourself. Break it, and you correct yourself and start again, as Aṅgulimāla did.
Q4 · the shape“3 – 4 – 3, like a phone number”3 by body, 4 by speech, 3 by mind. Speech gets the most because words do so much harm.
Q4 · the ten“KSS · Little Snakes Hiss Idly · 3 Cs”Kill Steal Sex · Lie Slander Harsh Idle · Covet, Curse (ill will), Confused (wrong) view.
Q5 · Buddha vs Jains“Jains watch the feet; the Buddha watches the heart”Jain monks sweep the path so as not to step on insects. The Buddha said what counts is the intention in the heart.
Q5 · the quote“Chetana-hum: I call kamma intention”Cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi: “Monks, it is intention that I call kamma.”
Q6 · the three“I Don't Own it”Impermanence (anicca) · Dukkha · nOt-self (anattā).
Q6 · how they link“Changes → Can't satisfy → Can't be me”What is impermanent is unsatisfactory; what is impermanent and unsatisfactory is not-self.
Q7 · the eight in order“Very Important Salads Always Leave Everyone Mindful & Calm”View, Intention (wisdom) · Speech, Action, Livelihood (morality) · Effort, Mindfulness, Concentration (concentration).
Q7 · the groups“2 – 3 – 3”Wisdom 2 · Morality 3 · Concentration 3. Total 8.
Q8 · why noble“Triple A: Any time, Anyone, Anywhere”True in America and Japan, in the Buddha's time and today, unlike “white” or “tall”, which depend on comparison.
Q8 · the four tasks“You Are Really Done”Understand suffering · Abandon craving · Realise cessation · Develop the path.
Q9 · the five“Funny Frogs Paint Fancy Castles”Form · Feeling · Perception · Formations · Consciousness.
Q9 · the similes“Foam Bubbles Make Bananas Magic”Foam (form) · bubble (feeling) · mirage (perception) · banana trunk (formations) · magic trick (consciousness). All look solid, none has…
Q10 · the two types“With body, without body”With remainder: the arahant still has a body (Buddha at 35). Without remainder: at final passing, nothing remains (Buddha at 80).
Q10 · what it ends“Blow out the three fires: Greed, Hate, Fog”Greed, hatred and delusion. Nibbāna = “blowing out”.
